Short answer: most of the 453 additions fit. Adding modern everyday words is what the real test intends; the old lists are just too small and too old. About 70 words don't match how the lists work, and they fall into five kinds. Each kind has its own tab with a suggestion per word. Every suggestion is pre-set; switch any word back to its current level if you disagree.

What each level is for (official)

N5Some basic Japanese from the classroom: set phrases and short, slow conversations about yourself, school and shopping.
N4Basic Japanese: short texts on familiar daily topics, everyday conversations spoken a little slowly.
N3The bridge. Everyday Japanese to a certain degree: concrete everyday texts, newspaper headlines, notices at school or work, near-natural conversation.
N2Everyday Japanese plus a wider range: newspaper and magazine articles on general topics, plain commentary, news reports, polite letters and requests from business partners.
N1Japanese in a wide range of situations: editorials and critiques, abstract and logically complex texts, lectures and news at natural speed.

How the real word list was built

  • JLPT has published no word lists since 2010. Its FAQ says a list wouldn't fit a test of what you can do with the language. Every list online, ours included, is Waller's adaptation of the pre-2010 lists.
  • The test makers did build a new internal table. A 2008 Japan Foundation report describes it: words picked mainly by frequency, then judged by experienced teachers, with the old list as a reference. Spoken words, onomatopoeia and loanwords got their own passes.
  • They aimed at 10,000–18,000 words in total, about twice the size of our lists. So filling the gap with common modern words fits the test's intent.
  • Their sample levels put everyday modern words low: うどん, エアコン, 朝ごはん at N5; ATM, アンケート, 一時停止 at N4; 駅ビル, いじめ, オープン at N3. Waller has アンケート at N1, so his katakana levels are a poor guide.
  • Affixes are entries of their own, marked with 〜 (〜的). Specialist terms "ordinary people don't need" are left out.

What our lists themselves show

  • Affixes are listed once (的, 性, 不, 全, 昨, 方), and predictable derivatives are mostly absent: only 8 〜的 words, 2 〜さ words and no 〜たち words in 8,000.
  • Prefix words are listed freely (最高, 不便, 全国, 前者, 今回, all N4–N3), and so are many 〜者 / 〜家 / 〜員 words (記者, 作家, 店員). Those additions fit.
  • Computer words sit at N4 (コンピューター, パソコン, ソフト) and N3 (情報, 通信, 操作). Job titles sit at N4–N3. Food sits at N5–N4.
  • History, war and religion aren't avoided: 軍隊, 宗教, 神, 城 N3; 武士, 天皇 N2. The N1 history and military additions fit.
  • Rough or spoken words are there too (お前, おい, あら, まあ N3), so あいつ, あんた, わし and the interjections fit.

What fits as it is

Modern everyday words (メール, チケット, 彼氏, お客様), news and society words at N2 (年金, 支援, 震災, 株価), editorial and abstract words at N1 (理念, 次元, 倫理, 陥る), interjections, and the history and military words. These match both the official level descriptions and how the lists treat similar words.
